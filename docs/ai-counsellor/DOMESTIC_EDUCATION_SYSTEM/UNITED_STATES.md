# United States — Domestic Education System

**Document purpose:** Reference knowledge so the GlobalyApp AI counsellor can interpret what a US student's stated education *means inside the US*, and — because the US is a major GlobalyApp destination — so the AI understands the receiving system's own structure precisely.
**Research date:** 2026-08-21
**Scope:** Domestic system only.

| ✅ | Confirmed against a Tier 1/Tier 2 source read during this research (source named inline) |
| ⚠ | Well-attested but only via secondary sources, or the official source exists and was not read in full — **must be confirmed at the named authority before the AI states it as a hard number** |

| [STABLE] / [PERIODIC] / [VOLATILE] | Change velocity |

> ## ⚠ THE FIVE THINGS THAT MOST OFTEN GO WRONG WITH THE UNITED STATES
>
> 1. **The US has no national qualifications framework, no national curriculum, and no national school-leaving examination.** *"Since the Constitution never mentions education, states hold primary legal authority over it."* ⚠ There is nothing in the US analogous to the AQF, SCQF, BNQF, NHEQF or Nepal's NQF.
> 2. **But the US DOES have a federally defined credit hour** — 34 CFR § 600.2 ✅. This is the closest thing the US has to a national academic unit, and it is precise. **Use it.**
> 3. **A US high school GPA may be "weighted" and exceed 4.0.** Weighted GPAs add bonus points for Honors/AP/IB courses ⚠. *"College registrars report the unweighted GPA on official transcripts"* ⚠ — so a "4.3 GPA" is a high-school weighted figure, not a college GPA.
> 4. **High school graduation credit requirements vary enormously** — *"Most U.S. states require between 18 and 26 credits"*, with some as low as **13** and some as high as **40** ⚠. A raw credit count says nothing without the state.
> 5. **"Accredited" in the US means accredited by an accreditor recognised by the U.S. Secretary of Education and/or CHEA** ✅ — the operative recognition question, and it is publicly checkable.

# 1. Country Education System Overview

## 1.1 Governance — the constitutional basis

| Item | Detail | Verification |
|------|--------|--------------|
| Constitutional basis | *"Under the **10th Amendment** to the Constitution, powers not granted to the federal government are reserved to the states. **Since the Constitution never mentions education, states hold primary legal authority over it.**"* | ⚠ (constitutional fact; corroborated across multiple sources) |
| State obligation | *"**Every state constitution requires the provision of a public school system**, granting state legislatures the primary responsibility for maintaining and operating schools, establishing curricula, setting graduation requirements, and regulating teacher qualifications."* | ⚠ |
| State bodies | *"Each state has a **State Board of Education** (or equivalent body) that develops education policies, sets curriculum standards, establishes graduation requirements, and oversees teacher certification."* | ⚠ |
| **Federal prohibition** | *"Federal statutes protect state control over public school curricula, **prohibiting the federal government from 'mandating, directing, or controlling' a state or local school board's 'instructional content' or 'curriculum.'**"* | ⚠ |
| Federal department | The **U.S. Department of Education (ED)** — *"aims to promote educational excellence and equal access and provides financial support to states"* | ⚠ |
| Historical note | *"the federal government did not issue any educational policy until the 1960s"* | ⚠ |
| Consequence | *"This is why education systems can look so different from one state to another."* | ⚠ |

> ⚠⚠ **The structural consequence for the AI:** the United States has **50 state systems plus DC and the territories**, with substantial **further variation at school-district level**. There is:
> - **no national curriculum** ⚠
> - **no national school-leaving examination** ⚠
> - **no national high school diploma** ⚠
> - **no national qualifications framework** ⚠
> - **no national grading scale** ⚠
>
> What the US *does* have nationally is: a **federally defined credit hour** (§8) ✅, a **federally recognised accreditation system** (§11) ✅, and **de facto national conventions** (the 4.0 GPA scale, the 120-credit bachelor's, the A–F letter grades) that are near-universal in practice but not legally mandated ⚠.

## 1.2 Local district control

> ⚠ *"Requirements for high school graduation vary by state, and in most cases, the state's education department sets graduation standards for the entire state. However, **a few states, such as Colorado, Massachusetts, and Pennsylvania, allow local school districts to determine most or all of the high school graduation requirements**."* ⚠

> ⚠ **AI note:** in a handful of states, the operative graduation requirement is the **school district's**, not the state's. So the question can be *"which district?"*, not just *"which state?"* ⚠

# 2. Education Levels — Actual Structure

```text

        ↓
KINDERGARTEN  ("K")  — age ~5 ⚠

ELEMENTARY SCHOOL   — typically Grades 1–5 (sometimes 1–6 or K–8) ⚠
        ↓
MIDDLE SCHOOL / JUNIOR HIGH — typically Grades 6–8 (sometimes 7–9) ⚠
        ↓
HIGH SCHOOL — typically Grades 9–12
        ├── Grade 9  = "freshman"
        ├── Grade 10 = "sophomore"
        ├── Grade 11 = "junior"
        └── Grade 12 = "senior"
        └── HIGH SCHOOL DIPLOMA

FOUR-YEAR COLLEGE / UNIVERSITY          TWO-YEAR / COMMUNITY COLLEGE
├── Bachelor's degree (~120 credits) ⚠  ├── Certificate ⚠
```

# 5. High School and the High School Diploma

**This is the qualification the AI will encounter most often from US students.**

| Item | Detail | Verification |
|------|--------|--------------|
| Grades | **9–12** ⚠ |
| Duration | **4 years** ⚠ |
| Ages | ~14–18 ⚠ |
| Credential | **High School Diploma** ⚠ |
| Who sets requirements | *"in most cases, the state's education department sets graduation standards for the entire state"*; but **Colorado, Massachusetts and Pennsylvania** *"allow local school districts to determine most or all"* of them ⚠ |
| **Credit requirement** | *"Most U.S. states require **between 18 and 26 credits** for a standard diploma"*; *"the most common number of credit hours is **20-24**"*; *"some states only require as few as **13** credits or course units on the low end, while others require as many as **40**"* ⚠ |
| Composition | *"earning a specific number of credit hours and taking courses in required academic subjects (like science and English) as well as electives"* ⚠ |
| **Non-credit requirements** | *"non-credit criteria for graduation can include **completing service hours, maintaining a minimum grade point average, or passing standardized tests**"* ⚠ |
| National examination | **None** ⚠ |

> ⚠⚠ **A US high school credit count is uninterpretable without the state.** A diploma earned with **13 credits** in one state and **40 credits** in another are **both complete diplomas**. ⚠ This is the same trap as Canada's provincial credit counts (30/80/100), but with a wider range and 50+ jurisdictions.
>
> **AI rule:** never treat a US high school credit total as a measure of achievement or volume relative to another state or country. Record the **state**, the **diploma type**, and the **GPA/transcript**.

# 7. Grading Systems

## 7.1 The letter-grade and 4.0 GPA convention ⚠

| Letter | Grade point (unweighted 4.0 scale) |
|--------|----------------------------------:|
| **A** | **4.0** |
| A− | 3.7 |
| B+ | 3.3 |
| **B** | **3.0** |
| B− | 2.7 |
| C+ | 2.3 ⚠ |
| **C** | **2.0** |
| C− | 1.7 ⚠ |
| D+ | 1.3 ⚠ |
| **D** | **1.0** |
| **F** | **0.0** |

⚠ *"On an unweighted 4.0 scale, A=4.0, B=3.0, C=2.0, D=1.0, F=0.0. A common mapping includes: A=4.0, A-=3.7, B+=3.3, B=3.0, B-=2.7 (**with small variations by school**)."* ⚠

> ⚠ **There is no national US grading scale.** The 4.0 scale with A–F letters is a **near-universal convention**, but the letter-to-point mapping, the percentage-to-letter mapping, and whether plus/minus grades are used at all are **institution-set**. ⚠

## 7.2 ⚠⚠ Weighted vs unweighted GPA — the defining US grading trap

| Type | Definition | Maximum |
|------|-----------|--------:|
| **Unweighted GPA** | All courses counted on the same 0.0–4.0 scale | **4.0** ⚠ |
| **Weighted GPA** | *"rewards academic rigor by adding bonus points for advanced classes — typically **+0.5 for Honors** and **+1.0 for AP or IB** courses"* | **can exceed 4.0** ⚠ |

**Two crucial sourced statements** ⚠:

> *"In the U.S., the most common scale is 0.0–4.0, while **some high schools also report a weighted GPA that can go above 4.0** to reflect Honors, AP, or IB courses."* ⚠

> *"**College registrars report the unweighted GPA on official transcripts.** Colleges typically **recalculate weighted GPAs back to unweighted** when comparing applicants, because **bonus-point rules differ across school districts**."* ⚠

> ⚠⚠ **AI rules for US GPAs:**
> 1. **A GPA above 4.0 is a high-school weighted GPA.** It is not a college GPA, and it is not an error.
> 2. **Weighted-GPA bonus rules differ by school district**, so two weighted 4.3 GPAs are not comparable. ⚠
> 3. **US colleges themselves recalculate to unweighted** when comparing applicants ⚠ — which is a strong signal that the AI should ask for the **unweighted** figure.
> 4. **College transcripts carry the unweighted GPA** ⚠ — so a college GPA should be ≤ 4.0.
> 5. **Always ask: high school or college? Weighted or unweighted? Out of what maximum?**

## 7.3 Latin honours ⚠

| Honour | Meaning | Typical GPA (⚠ institution-specific) |
|--------|---------|--------------------------------------|
| **cum laude** | *"with honor"* | ~**3.5–3.7** ⚠ |
| **magna cum laude** | *"with great honor"* | ~**3.7–3.9** ⚠ |
| **summa cum laude** | *"with highest honor"* | ~**3.9+** ⚠ |

> ⚠⚠ **Directly from the source, and important:** *"**The ranges of GPAs that correspond with which category differ from institution to institution.** While a 4.0 GPA may constitute a summa cum laude honor at almost every school, **a 3.79 GPA may qualify as cum laude at one college and no honor at another**."* ⚠
>
> **So Latin honours are institution-relative.** They confirm strong performance but do **not** pin down a GPA. The AI should record the honour **and** ask for the GPA. ⚠

⚠ Note also that many US institutions award Latin honours by **class rank percentile** rather than a fixed GPA threshold. ❌ *Not verified in this pass.*

|-----------|--------|

# 8. The Credit System — the one thing the US defines federally ✅

**This is the most valuable US fact in this document, and it comes from federal regulation.**

## 8.1 The federal definition of a credit hour — 34 CFR § 600.2 ✅

**Verbatim:**

> *"Except as provided in 34 CFR 668.8(k) and (l), a **credit hour** is an amount of student work defined by an institution, as approved by the institution's accrediting agency or State approval agency, that is consistent with commonly accepted practice in postsecondary education and that—*
>
> *(1) Reasonably approximates not less than—*
>
> *(i) **One hour of classroom or direct faculty instruction and a minimum of two hours of out-of-class student work each week for approximately fifteen weeks for one semester or trimester hour of credit, or ten to twelve weeks for one quarter hour of credit**, or the equivalent amount of work over a different period of time; or*
>
> *(ii) At least an equivalent amount of work as required in paragraph (1)(i) of this definition for **other academic activities as established by the institution, including laboratory work, internships, practica, studio work, and other academic work** leading to the award of credit hours; and*
>
> *(2) Permits an institution, in determining the amount of work associated with a credit hour, to take into account **a variety of delivery methods, measurements of student work, academic calendars, disciplines, and degree levels**."* ✅

## 8.2 What the definition establishes ✅

| Element | Value |
|---------|------:|
| **Classroom/direct faculty instruction per credit per week** | **1 hour** ✅ |
| **Minimum out-of-class student work per credit per week** | **2 hours** ✅ |
| **Total per credit per week** | **~3 hours** ✅ |
| **Semester / trimester length** | **~15 weeks** ✅ |
| **Quarter length** | **10–12 weeks** ✅ |
| **Therefore: 1 semester credit ≈** | **~45 notional hours** (3 hrs × 15 weeks) ✅ *(arithmetic from the regulation)* |
| **Semester ↔ quarter conversion** | *"1 semester hour = 1.5 quarter hours"* ⚠ (secondary source, but arithmetically consistent with 15 vs 10 weeks) |

## 8.3 The Carnegie unit ⚠

> ⚠ *"The definition approximates the original **time-based Carnegie unit** (one hour of classroom instruction and two hours of student work outside the classroom), but instructs colleges to evaluate that approximation based on the '**amount of work represented in intended learning outcomes**' as measured by '**evidence of student achievement**'."* ⚠

⚠ **The "Carnegie unit"** is also the traditional unit of **high school** credit (typically one year of a subject meeting daily) ⚠. ❌ *The high school Carnegie unit's definition — not verified in this pass. **Relevant**, because US high school "credits" are Carnegie-unit based and therefore a different unit from college credit hours.*

## 8.4 ⚠⚠ Cross-country credit comparison

| System | Notional hours per credit | Credits per year |
|--------|--------------------------:|-----------------:|
| **US semester credit hour** | **~45** ✅ (1 + 2 hrs × 15 weeks) | ~30 ⚠ (120 ÷ 4 years) |
| **UK CATS / SCQF** | **10** ✅ | 120 ✅ |
| **ECTS** | **25–30** ✅ | 60 ⚠ |
| **India (NCrF)** | **30** ✅ | **40** ✅ |
| **Bangladesh (BNQF)** | **40** (lecture) ✅ | not fixed |
| **Nepal (Grade 11–12)** | not defined nationally ⚠ | ~27 ⚠ |
| **Australia** | **EFTSL** (1.0 = one full-time year); credit points institution-set ⚠ | — |
| **Canada** | **not defined in notional hours** ✅ (absence confirmed in the CDQF) | 90–120 for a degree ✅ |
| **China** | **not established** ❌ | — |

> ⚠⚠ **This table is the single most important anti-fabrication tool in this research project.**
>
> A **US 120-credit bachelor's degree** (~5,400 notional hours by the federal arithmetic) and a **UK 360-credit bachelor's honours degree** (3,600 notional hours by CATS) and an **Indian 120-credit bachelor's degree** (3,600 notional hours by NCrF) all describe legitimate first degrees — **and the credit numbers are not comparable at all.** The US and Indian degrees show the *same credit number* while representing *different notional volumes*; the US and UK degrees show *different credit numbers* (120 vs 360) for broadly *comparable* study.
>
> **AI rule: NEVER compare raw credit counts across countries.** Where a comparison is genuinely needed, reason in **years of full-time study** or **notional hours**, state the assumption, and defer the formal judgement to the destination institution or a recognised evaluator.

# 10. Qualification Framework

## 10.1 ⚠⚠ The United States has no national qualifications framework

> ⚠ **This is a genuine and important structural finding.** Unlike Australia (AQF), the UK (RQF/FHEQ/SCQF/CQFW), Nepal (NQF), Bangladesh (BNQF) and India (NHEQF/NCrF), the United States has:

> - **no national level descriptors**
> - **no national qualification-type register**
> - **no national body that places a qualification on a level**

> ❌ *The absence was **not positively confirmed** from an official source in this pass, and should be. # 16. Academic Pathways

**Main academic pathway:**
```text
Pre-K (optional) → Kindergarten
  → Elementary (Grades 1–5, variable)
  → Middle / junior high (Grades 6–8, variable)
  → High school (Grades 9–12: freshman, sophomore, junior, senior)
       → HIGH SCHOOL DIPLOMA (state/district credit requirements; 13–40 credits) ⚠
       → [SAT / ACT, where required]
  → Bachelor's degree (~120 credits, ~4 years) ⚠
  → Master's degree (30–60 credits, 1–2 years) ⚠
  → Doctoral degree (60–90 credits coursework + dissertation, 4–7 years) ⚠
```

**Community college / "2+2" pathway:**
```text
High School Diploma (or GED/HiSET)
  → Community college (open access)
       ├── Certificate → employment
       └── ASSOCIATE DEGREE (~60 credits, ~2 years)
              → transfer to a four-year institution with credit
              → BACHELOR'S DEGREE (remaining ~60 credits)
```
⚠ Credit acceptance is at the receiving institution's discretion; **articulation agreements** and **statewide transfer systems** formalise it in many states ⚠.

**Equivalency pathway:**
```text
No high school diploma
  → GED (four subject tests) or HiSET
  → community college or (institution-permitting) a four-year institution
```
✅ The GED is explicitly *"an alternative to the U.S. high school diploma"* ✅.

**Professional pathway:**
```text
Bachelor's degree (any field, subject to prerequisites)
  → LSAT → Juris Doctor (JD, ~83–90 credits, 3 years) ⚠
  → MCAT → Doctor of Medicine (MD, 4 years) ⚠
  → DAT → DDS/DMD ⚠ ; PharmD, DVM, OD, DPT ⚠
```
⚠ **All US professional doctorates are post-bachelor's**, which is the structurally significant fact — not the "Doctor" in the title.

**Advanced-standing pathway:**
```text
High school + AP examinations / IB Diploma / dual enrolment
  → college credit awarded at matriculation (institution-specific) ⚠
  → shortened bachelor's degree ⚠
```

# 18. International Recognition Context (descriptive only)

**What exactly is a "US high school diploma"?**

- A credential awarded by a **school**, under **state** (or in some states **district**) graduation requirements — **not** a national qualification, and **not** based on a national examination. ⚠
- It represents **12 years of schooling after kindergarten** (13 including kindergarten) ⚠, with the final four years (**Grades 9–12**) constituting high school ⚠.
- Requirements are expressed in **credits (Carnegie units)**: *"Most U.S. states require between **18 and 26 credits**"*, most commonly **20–24**, with a national range from **13 to 40** ⚠.
- Additional requirements may include **service hours, a minimum GPA, and/or passing standardized tests** ⚠.
- The accompanying **transcript** carries letter grades and a **GPA on a 4.0 scale** — which may be **weighted (above 4.0)** or **unweighted** ⚠.
- **The GED and HiSET are explicit alternatives to it**, not the same credential ✅.
- **There is no US national qualifications framework**, so no US level can be stated. ⚠

**What exactly is a "US bachelor's degree"?**
- A degree of **~120 semester credit hours** (range 120–132) ⚠, typically **four years**, awarded by an institution whose accreditation comes from a **USDE- and/or CHEA-recognised accreditor** ✅. A **US semester credit hour** is federally defined as ≈ **1 hour of instruction + 2 hours of out-of-class work per week for ~15 weeks** ✅ (≈45 notional hours). It typically includes substantial **general-education breadth requirements** outside the major ⚠.

**What exactly is a "US associate degree"?**
- A **~60-credit, ~2-year** qualification ⚠, typically from a **community college**, which functions **both** as a terminal credential **and** as the first half of a bachelor's degree via transfer ⚠.

**What exactly is a "US JD" or "MD"?**
- A **doctoral-titled professional degree entered after a bachelor's degree** ⚠ — the JD typically **~83–90 semester credits over 3 years** ⚠, the MD typically 4 years with institution-specific credit accounting ⚠. **Because the US has no qualifications framework, no US level can be stated** — unlike Canada (bachelor's-level in academic standing ✅) or the UK (FHEQ 7 ✅).

**What exactly is a "US GPA"?**
- A credit-weighted average on a **4.0 scale** ⚠. **A high school GPA may be weighted and exceed 4.0**; **a college GPA on an official transcript is unweighted** ⚠. **The letter-to-point mapping varies slightly by institution** ⚠, and there is **no national scale**. ⚠

*Cross-country equivalency is deliberately out of scope for this document.*

# 21. United States-Specific Complexity Summary

The six things that most often go wrong when interpreting a US student's education:

1. **There is no US qualifications framework** — so no US level can be stated. Describe by type, credits, duration, entry requirement and accreditation instead. (§10)
2. **A high school GPA above 4.0 is weighted**; a college GPA on a transcript is unweighted. Always ask which. (§7.2)
3. **High school credit requirements range from 13 to 40 across states** — a raw credit count says nothing without the state. (§5)
4. **"Diploma" in US usage means the certificate document**, not a sub-degree qualification. (§12)
5. **"College" means higher education in the US** — the opposite of the Nepal/Bangladesh/UK/Canada senses. (§9.1)
6. **"Accredited" means accredited by a USDE/CHEA-recognised accreditor** — a real, checkable, and occasionally failed test. (§11)

Plus: the **federally defined credit hour** (≈45 notional hours per semester credit) is the one precise national US unit and must not be equated with UK, ECTS, Indian or Bangladeshi credits (§8); **JD and MD have no US framework level** even though Canada and the UK place their counterparts explicitly (§9.2); and **"freshman/sophomore/junior/senior"** are ambiguous between high school and college (§2.2).
